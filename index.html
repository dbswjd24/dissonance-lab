Dissonance Lab

Eileen Hwang · Music Perception & Cognition final project · Fall 2026

Overview Curve Chords Study Notes

Some intervals sound smooth and others sound harsh. One explanation is mechanical. Two partials that land close together on the basilar membrane interfere, and we hear that as roughness (Plomp & Levelt, 1965). The other explanation is learned. We like the intervals our musical culture taught us to like.

This site implements the first explanation as a model and tests it against people. If the model matches non-musicians better than musicians, that suggests training adds something on top of the ear.

Notes

Models

Sethares (1993). A fit to Plomp and Levelt's curve. For two partials with amplitudes a₁, a₂ and frequency difference Δf,

d = min(a₁, a₂) · ( e−3.51 s Δf − e−5.75 s Δf ), s = 0.24 / (0.0207 fmin + 18.96)

Hutchinson & Knopoff (1978). Roughness peaks at a quarter of a critical band:

g(y) = ( e · 4y · e−4y )², y = Δf / CB(f̄)

CB can be Zwicker and Terhardt's critical bandwidth, 25 + 75(1 + 1.4(f/1000)²)0.69, or Glasberg and Moore's ERB, 24.7(4.37f/1000 + 1), the formula given in Unit 4.

Ratio baseline. Tenney height, log₂(p·q) for an interval p/q. It isn't a hearing model. It stands in for the idea that we judge intervals by how simple their ratios are.

What the model doesn't include

  • Context: the same dyad in a chord progression versus in isolation.
  • Stream segregation (Wright & Bregman), which can mask dissonance between separate voices.
  • Training and enculturation. This is the part the study tries to measure.
  • Loudness, masking, and the ear's frequency response. Amplitudes are used as given.
