How it works

Ear or experience?

Cognitive science often asks how perception mixes bottom-up sensory signals with top-down knowledge. Consonance is a clear test case. The sensory account says two partials that land in the same critical band of the basilar membrane interact, and we hear that interaction as roughness (Plomp & Levelt, 1965). The learning account says we find intervals pleasant because our musical culture taught us to. This site builds the sensory model and tests it against listeners.

The models

  • Sethares (1993). d = min(a₁,a₂)·(e−3.51·s·Δf − e−5.75·s·Δf) with s = 0.24 / (0.0207·fmin + 18.96).
  • Hutchinson & Knopoff (1978). g(y) = (e·4y·e−4y)², where y is the frequency difference in critical bandwidths. Uses either the Zwicker critical band, 25 + 75(1 + 1.4(f/1000)²)0.69, or the ERB, 24.7(4.37f/1000 + 1).
  • Simple-ratio baseline. Tenney height, log₂(p·q), stands in for learned knowledge about interval ratios.

What it predicts

  • Pure tones stop clashing beyond about a minor third. Rich tones have smooth spots at simple ratios (2/1, 3/2, 4/3, 5/4).
  • Stretch the partials and the "octave" moves with them, so tuning follows timbre (Sethares, 2005).
  • The same interval is rougher low in the bass, where critical bands are wider.
  • It ignores context, training, and stream segregation. Where it misses, top-down factors are the likely cause.
